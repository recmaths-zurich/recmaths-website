Pole Dancing Problem Pole Dancing Problem

Imagine you're on a disk starting in the center. There are $n$ poles on the disk. You have a coil of yarn (for this purpose it has infinite length). The end of the yarn is fixed to the middle of the disk. While you walk the disk the yarn is stretched from the starting point to you.

Your goal is to sweep all of the area. More precisely, have the stretched yarn pass over every point. The difficult thing now is that whenever the yarn hits a pole it wraps around it. Therefore the yarn is now stretched between you and the pole and not between you and the center. Of course, if you retrace your steps, the yarn can unwind from the pole again.

The question now is: What is the shortest path $L(n)$ to do this in terms of $n$? Try it out in this interactive simulation:

In an attempt to make it a little more formal, we could say that a path $\gamma : [0, 1] \rightarrow \mathbb{D}$ with $\gamma(0)=(0,0)$, where $\mathbb{D}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^2 : |x|\le 1 \}$ is the unit disk in $\mathbb{R}^2$, is called sweeping if following it while holding the yarn attached to the center 'sweeps' the entire area of the disk.

Let $\mathbb{P}_n=\{P \subseteq \mathbb{D} : |P|=n\}$ be the set of all configurations of $n$ poles. We can then define a function $f : n \longmapsto \sup_{P\in\mathbb{P}_n}$ $\inf_{\gamma\, \text{sweeping}} L(\gamma)$, thus $f(n)$ is how long the best sweeping path is with the most restrictive placement of the poles. Perhaps a good question: what do these restricting pole placements look like?

Assume there are $0$ poles. You can walk to a side and then go around in a circle, sweeping the entire disk. Since this is clearly optimal, $f(0)=2\pi+1$. We can also show $f(2)=f(1)=f(0)=2\pi+1$, but $f(3)>f(2)$ (think about these!). But how does $f$ grow? Maybe $f\in\Theta(\sqrt n)$?
